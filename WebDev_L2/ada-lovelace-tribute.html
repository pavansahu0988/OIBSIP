Ada Lovelace

The Enchantress of Numbers — the mathematician who saw a future in the machine before anyone else did.

1815 – 1852

Her story

A mind ahead of its century

Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace, was born in London in 1815, the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron. Her mother, Annabella Milbanke, separated from Byron soon after Ada's birth and, wary of the "poetic madness" she associated with her husband, steered her daughter firmly toward mathematics and logic. Tutors including Mary Somerville and Augustus De Morgan shaped a young woman who came to describe her own approach as "poetical science" — a fusion of rigorous analysis and imaginative leap that would define her life's work.

In 1833, at just seventeen, Ada met the inventor Charles Babbage at a demonstration of his Difference Engine, a mechanical calculator. The two struck up a friendship and correspondence that would last the rest of her life, built on a shared fascination with Babbage's far grander, never-completed design: the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose mechanical computer decades before electricity made such machines practical.

Between 1842 and 1843, Ada translated an Italian engineer's academic paper on the Analytical Engine into English. Where the original ran to a few thousand words, her translation arrived with a set of appended notes nearly three times as long. Buried within them, labeled simply "Note G," was a step-by-step method for the engine to compute Bernoulli numbers — widely regarded today as the first algorithm ever written for execution by a machine.

What set Ada apart was not just the algorithm but the insight surrounding it. She argued that Babbage's engine could go beyond arithmetic entirely — manipulating symbols, composing music, doing anything that could be expressed in the machine's formal language. It was a conceptual leap that would not be fully realized for over a century, and it is the reason so many now consider her the first person to grasp the true potential of computing. She died in 1852, at just thirty-six, and was buried, at her own request, beside the father she never really knew.

Milestones

A life in brief

1815

Born in London

Daughter of poet Lord Byron and mathematician Annabella Milbanke; raised on logic and science rather than verse.

1833

Meets Charles Babbage

At seventeen, sees a demonstration of the Difference Engine and begins a lifelong intellectual friendship with its inventor.

1842–43

Translates and annotates

Her English translation of Menabrea's paper on the Analytical Engine grows into a body of original notes far longer than the source.

1843

Publishes "Note G"

Sets out a method for the Analytical Engine to compute Bernoulli numbers — recognized today as the first published algorithm for a machine.

1852

Dies at thirty-six

Passes away in London and is buried beside her father, Lord Byron, as she had requested.

1980

The Ada programming language

The U.S. Department of Defense names a newly developed programming language "Ada" in her honor.

Why she matters

Her legacy today

First programmer

Her Bernoulli number algorithm is widely cited as the first computer program, written for a machine that was never built in her lifetime.

A wider vision

She imagined computing beyond arithmetic — an idea that took over a hundred years for the field to catch up to.

Ada Lovelace Day

Celebrated each October, it honors women in science and technology in her name, keeping her story part of the present.
